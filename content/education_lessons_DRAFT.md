## Series A — Bankroll basics

### 1. Your bankroll is not your rent money
Your bankroll is money you can afford to lose entirely, set aside from bills, savings and groceries. Decide the number before you place a bet, and treat it as spent entertainment budget that happens to have upside. If losing it would change your month, it's too big. A smaller bankroll doesn't make you less serious; it makes every decision calmer, and calm decisions are the whole game.

### 2. Why flat betting beats guessing
Betting a different amount on every game based on "feel" ties your results to your mood. Flat betting means risking the same small slice of your bankroll on each pick. It keeps one bad night from erasing weeks, and it makes your results measurable: you can tell whether the process works, not whether you got lucky on a big bet.

### 3. What a "unit" really is
A unit is a fixed fraction of your bankroll, commonly 1%. With a $1,000 bankroll, 1 unit is $10. Units let everyone talk about results without talking about dollars: "+4 units" means the same thing whether your unit is $5 or $500. Pick a unit size you won't flinch at when a bet loses, and keep it for the whole season.

### 4. The 1–2% rule
Most disciplined bettors risk 1–2% of their bankroll per bet. The reason is math, not caution: even a good bettor hits losing streaks of 8–10 bets. At 2% per bet, ten straight losses cost about 18% of your bankroll, painful but survivable. At 10% per bet, the same streak wipes out roughly two-thirds. Small stakes keep you in the game long enough for an edge to show up.

### 5. Variance: why good bets lose
A bet at 55% to win still loses 45% of the time. Over 20 bets you'd expect around 11 wins, but 8 or 14 wouldn't be surprising. Variance is the gap between what should happen and what does in a small sample. It's why one losing week proves nothing, and why judging a strategy takes hundreds of bets, not dozens.

### 6. Sizing up with Kelly (the gentle version)
The Kelly formula suggests a stake based on your edge: bigger edge, bigger bet. Full Kelly is far too aggressive because our probabilities are estimates, not facts. Most bettors use a fraction, like a quarter of Kelly, which keeps the idea (size follows edge) without the wild swings. When we suggest 0.5u, 1u or 1.5u, that's the thinking behind it.

### 7. Never raise stakes to "get back to even"
After a loss, the urge is to bet bigger so one win erases it. That's the fastest way to turn a bad week into a ruined bankroll. Your edge on the next bet is the same as on any other bet; it doesn't know what happened yesterday. Keep your stake the same after a loss as after a win.

### 8. Track every bet, including the ugly ones
Write down every bet: date, pick, odds, stake, result. People who track find out what's really working. People who don't remember their wins and forget their losses, and make decisions based on a story that isn't true. A simple spreadsheet is enough. Honest records are the foundation for everything else.

## Series B — Discipline and mindset

### 9. Chasing losses is the real enemy
Chasing means betting more, or betting worse bets, to recover what you lost. It feels like fixing a problem; it's actually making it larger. If you notice yourself placing bets you didn't plan to place, that's the signal to stop for the day. The best defence is deciding your bets in the morning, before emotions get a vote.

### 10. Set a daily and weekly stop
Decide in advance the most you're willing to lose in a day and a week, then stick to it. When you hit the number, you're done until the next period. A stop-loss isn't admitting defeat; it's the rule that protects the money you'll want to have when your edge does show up.

### 11. Don't bet because you're bored
Late-night, nothing-on, "I'll just put a little on this" bets are rarely selective bets. Good bettors pass on most games. A day with no qualifying pick is a perfectly good day. If you're betting mainly to have something to watch, set that spending aside as entertainment and keep it separate from your bankroll.

### 12. Parlays: fun, but expensive
Every extra leg multiplies the sportsbook's built-in margin. A four-leg parlay can carry a house edge several times larger than a single bet at the same quality. If you enjoy parlays, treat them as lottery tickets with a small, fixed budget, and keep your serious bankroll on single bets.

### 13. Recency bias: last night isn't a trend
A team that just won by 20 isn't necessarily better than it was; one result is mostly noise. Markets already price in recent form, so betting on a hot streak often means paying extra for a story. Look at larger samples (10 games, not 1) and ask whether the number already accounts for what you saw.

### 14. Cheering for your team clouds your math
Betting on your favourite team is the hardest bet to evaluate honestly. Fandom inflates how good you think they are. Either avoid betting your own team entirely, or hold those bets to the same rules as any other game: same stake, same edge requirement, no exceptions.

## Series C — Understanding odds and +EV

### 15. How to read American odds
-150 means you risk $150 to win $100. +130 means you risk $100 to win $130. Negative numbers are favourites, positive numbers are underdogs. Odds are also a statement of probability: -150 implies about a 60% chance, +130 about 43%. Learning to convert between the two is the single most useful skill in betting.

### 16. Implied probability: what the price is telling you
Every price has a break-even win rate built in. At -110, you need to win 52.4% of the time just to break even. If you believe the true chance is higher than the price implies, you may have an edge; if not, you're paying for the entertainment. Always ask: "What does this price need to be true?"

### 17. What "+EV" actually means
EV, expected value, is the average result per bet if you could place it thousands of times. A +EV bet isn't one that wins; it's one whose price is better than its true odds. A 4% EV bet is expected to return $4 for every $100 risked over the long run, while still losing on any single night. Value, not winning, is the goal.

### 18. The vig: how books get paid
The "vig" is the margin sportsbooks build into prices. A two-way market at -110 / -110 implies 104.8% total probability, so that extra 4.8% is the book's cut. Removing it gives the "no-vig" price, the market's honest view of the odds. Our picks start from that no-vig number, which is why we can say how much edge a price really has.

### 19. Why sharp books set the benchmark
Some sportsbooks welcome large bets from skilled bettors and move their prices in response. Their prices tend to be the most accurate, and softer books lag behind. When a soft book's price is better than the sharp consensus, that gap is where +EV bets come from. It's less about predicting games and more about finding mispriced numbers.

### 20. Moneyline, spread, total: what each one is
A moneyline is picking the winner. A spread gives or takes points to even out the matchup. A total (over/under) is betting on the combined score. All three can have edges, and each has its own quirks: spreads depend on margin, totals on pace and scoring. Our picks always tell you which market it is and why.

### 21. Key numbers and half-points
In football, games land on 3 and 7 far more often than on 4 or 5, so a spread moving from -3 to -3.5 matters much more than -4 to -4.5. In hockey and baseball, half-runs and half-goals matter more than the headline price. Always check the exact number: half a point can be worth several percent of EV.

### 22. Why a bet can be +EV and still lose today
The math says a 55% bet wins more than it loses over time, not that it wins tonight. That's why we publish everything, the losses included. Judge the process by the long-run record and by closing-line value, not by any single result.

## Series D — Process and edge

### 23. Line shopping is free money
The same bet can be priced differently at different books: -110 at one, -105 at another. That 5-cent difference adds roughly 2% to your expected return, forever, for no extra risk. Having accounts at multiple books and checking before you bet is the simplest edge available to every bettor.

### 24. What closing line value (CLV) tells you
The closing line is the final price before a game starts, usually the market's most accurate. If you consistently bet at better prices than the closing line, you're probably finding real value, even through losing streaks. That's why we track CLV on every pick: it's an early signal, far less noisy than win-loss.

### 25. When the market agrees with you
If the price moves in your direction after you identify a bet, the market is confirming your view. That's usually a good sign, though it also means the price may have got worse by the time you bet. When we say "the market agrees," we're showing that sharp money is leaning the same way as our numbers.

### 26. When the line moves against you
A price drifting away from your side can mean the market knows something you don't, such as an injury or lineup news. It can also be noise. We use line movement as one of three tests: if a price has moved sharply against a pick since the open, we pass on it rather than hope.

### 27. Why we skip days (and sports)
We publish a pick only if it passes three tests: the math, the team's recent and head-to-head history, and the line movement. Some days nothing passes, and we say so. Passing on weak bets is what makes the good ones meaningful, and it protects your bankroll on days with no real edge.

### 28. Small samples lie, large samples tell the truth
Fifty bets can't tell you whether a strategy works; even 200 are a modest sample. Win rates swing wildly early on. Instead of asking "am I up?", ask "am I beating the closing line?" and "am I following my rules?" Those are the questions that hold up over a hundred bets.

## Series E — Responsible play

### 29. Know the warning signs
Betting more than you planned, hiding bets from people close to you, borrowing to bet, feeling anxious when you can't bet, or chasing losses are all warning signs. If any ring true, step back. Most sportsbooks offer deposit limits, cool-off periods and self-exclusion, and using them is a sign of control, not weakness.

### 30. Help is available
If gambling stops being fun, support is free and confidential. In the US you can call or text 1-800-GAMBLER (1-800-426-2537) any time, and many states offer their own helplines. No pick, no edge and no bankroll is worth more than your wellbeing. You must be of legal betting age in your state, and betting always involves risk of loss.
